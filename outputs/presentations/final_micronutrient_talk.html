Welcome — today I’m going to walk you through a systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis we completed this year on micronutrient supplementation for depression and anxiety. I want to give you a clinically useful take on what the evidence actually supports — and just as importantly, what it doesn’t.

Your patients are likely already asking this

  • “Should I take vitamin D for my mood?”
  • “I read that magnesium helps anxiety”
  • “My functional medicine doctor put me on B12”

But most reviews lump everything together as “supplements,” making it difficult to advise individual patients.

The clinical motivation is real. I’d guess most of you have fielded questions like these in clinic. The problem is the literature is genuinely hard to interpret — fish oil, turmeric, vitamins all get grouped under “nutritional psychiatry.” Our goal was to cut through that by looking at individual micronutrients, in only the highest-quality trials.

How to compare across studies?

What’s a network meta-analysis?

Network meta-analysis
One model compares all nutrients together
  • Lets us estimate comparisons even without head-to-head trials
Bayesian random-effects model
The model estimates the average effect and the disagreement between studies
  • μ = the pooled average treatment effect
  • τ = how much true effects vary across studies

Let’s get to the results

Depression: the main signals

Vitamin D is the most stable signal because its interval excludes zero with more support. Selenium also excludes zero, but the evidence base is much sparser, so it is intriguing rather than practice-informing. 2 studies with only 122 people. Also τ = 0.45, 95% CrI: 0.29 to 0.63, so there is large heterogeneity across studies.

Selenium may affect depression symptoms through multiple mechanisms, including antioxidant activity via selenoenzymes, modulation of neurotransmitter systems, anti-inflammatory effects, and regulation of neuronal signaling pathways

Vitamin B6 affects depression symptoms primarily through its role as a cofactor in neurotransmitter synthesis, particularly as pyridoxal 5’-phosphate (PLP), the active coenzyme form that is essential for producing serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, and GABA.

The primary mechanism involves vitamin D receptor signaling in the central nervous system. VDR is widely expressed in brain regions critical for mood regulation, including the hippocampus, hypothalamus, and prefrontal cortex.

Does baseline deficiency change the picture for vitamin D?

“We had reported a mean drug-placebo difference of 1.80 points on the HAM-D and a standardized mean difference (SMD) of 0.32. The differences reported in the replications ranged from 1.62 to 2.56 HAM-D points, with SMD effect sizes ranging from 0.23 to 0.34. To put this into perspective, the NICE criteria for clinical significance of antidepressant-placebo differences are three points on the HAM-D or SMDs of at least 0.50, corresponding to what Cohen (17) proposed as a moderate effect size.” https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00407/full?trk=public_post_comment-text

The deficiency-informed framework

Important caveat: this is study-level, not patient-level evidence. The subgroup pattern is clinically suggestive, but it does not prove that an individual patient will benefit simply because they are deficient.

What the subgroup data support

This is the conceptual contribution I’m most proud of. The reason the literature is hard to interpret is that most trials enroll whoever has depression — deficient and replete mixed together. When you average across those groups, you dilute the real signal from the deficient subgroup. The framework: check first, supplement if there’s a biochemical reason to.

What about augmentation?

5 augmentation studies. These were modeled separately from the placebo-controlled network, and all credible intervals still cross zero, so the signal is suggestive rather than definitive.

5 studies used micronutrients as augmentation on top of antidepressants. We modeled these separately — can’t compare vitamin D vs. placebo with vitamin D + antidepressant vs. antidepressant alone in the same network. Point estimates are in the right direction but every credible interval crosses zero. Worth watching, not actionable yet.

Anxiety: no clear signal

No micronutrient reached a credible interval excluding zero

For anxiety, the picture is starker. Nothing crosses the line. The forest plot is much sparser — only 7 rows vs. 14 for depression. Selenium has the largest point estimate at −0.24 but the interval is wide. Vitamin D, which drove the depression signal, shows essentially no effect here. All comparisons were rated very low confidence by CINeMA. This may be an underpowered literature, not a definitive null.

Why anxiety might have lagged

  • Only 18 studies assessed anxiety vs. 50 for depression
  • Substantial heterogeneity (τ = 0.34, 95% CrI: 0.05 to 0.68)
  • Vitamin D — which drove the depression signal — showed essentially no effect on anxiety
  • Most vitamin D trials enrolled older adults with depression, not anxiety disorders

The anxiety question is genuinely open — underpowered literature, not a definitive null

The anxiety finding is less “micronutrients don’t work for anxiety” and more “we haven’t studied this well enough.” Fewer trials, more heterogeneity, different participant profiles. The anxiety literature needs purpose-built trials.

Safety

  • No micronutrient was associated with a credible increase in adverse events

  • All odds ratio credible intervals included the null

  • Based on 33 of 50 low-RoB studies (64.7%) with available safety data

  • “Seems safe” ≠ indiscriminate
  • Vitamin D: hypercalcemia at high doses
  • B6: neuropathy long-term at high dose
  • Drug-nutrient interactions
  • “Natural” ≠ harmless

The safety picture is reassuring — no micronutrient associated with a credible increase in adverse events. But I want to push back on the framing that supplements are inherently benign. High-dose vitamin D can cause hypercalcemia. Long-term high-dose B6 has been linked to peripheral neuropathy. Clinical judgment still applies.

What this study is not saying

❌ “Vitamins treat depression”

❌ “All supplements are useless”

❌ “Check levels on every patient”

❌ “Natural means safe, try it”

✅ Vitamin D has modest credible evidence for depressive symptoms, especially in deficient patients

✅ For most other nutrients: too weak to act on

✅ Targeted deficiency assessment may have a role in selected patients

I want to be explicit about what we can and can’t conclude. The evidence doesn’t support writing vitamin D on every depressed patient’s problem list. It supports thinking carefully about whether a specific patient might be deficient — and if so, correcting that has the best evidence base we found.

Limitations

  • High heterogeneity across all networks (τ = 0.45 for depression)
    • Prediction intervals are wide even when point estimates look promising
  • Baseline biomarker data inconsistently reported in ~22% of studies
    • Limits our ability to test deficiency-modification across most nutrients
  • Study-level, not patient-level subgroup analyses
    • More vulnerable to ecological confounding
  • Sparse evidence for most nutrients beyond vitamin D
    • Wide credible intervals do not equal proof of no effect
  • Low to very low confidence for most comparisons (CINeMA)
    • As always, better research needed

I don’t want to oversell this. The heterogeneity is real. The subgroup data are study-level — a weaker form of evidence. And for most nutrients outside vitamin D, we simply don’t have enough good studies to draw firm conclusions in either direction. Tau did reduce accounting for baseline status, and removing Iranian studies.

What to do Monday morning

  • Patient asks about supplements for depression: → “Evidence is strongest for vitamin D — but only if you’re deficient”

  • Consider checking vitamin D in depressed patients at higher risk for deficiency → Older adults, limited sun exposure, darker skin, malabsorption

  • Don’t recommend broad supplementation — evidence isn’t there yet

  • Patients already taking supplements: counsel on dose limits; more isn’t always better

The practical close. A framework for answering the question your patients are already asking. Vitamin D in the right patient, with the right indication. Everything else: honest uncertainty.
